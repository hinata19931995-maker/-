# Scene Strategy for Product Development

## Objective

Use a concrete usage scene as a product-development input, not merely as a marketing description.

A scene should help define:

- who has the need
- when and where the need is activated
- what the user is trying to do
- how often it happens
- what constraints shape the experience
- what failure or friction matters
- what role the product should play

## Scene Definition

For important product decisions, describe a scene with:

- Market / geography
- User group
- Place
- Time / timing
- Trigger
- Action sequence
- Frequency
- Desired outcome
- Environmental constraints
- Current solution / substitute
- Friction / failure

## Four Core Scene Variables

When useful, explicitly analyze:

1. **Timing** — when the need becomes active
2. **Occasion / Place** — the physical or social setting
3. **Action** — what the user is actually doing
4. **Frequency** — how often the behavior occurs

These variables help distinguish a real recurring use case from a vague lifestyle label.

## Scene Roles

Classify scenes by their role in the product strategy.

### Core Scene

The scene that most strongly explains why the product should exist.

Characteristics often include:

- high job importance
- meaningful frequency
- strong problem consequence or friction
- clear user value
- strong product fit

The product should be optimized around this scene first.

### Supporting Scene

A secondary scene that expands usefulness without distorting the core product.

Supporting scenes may justify compatibility, portability, flexibility, or accessory requirements, but should not override the core scene unless evidence becomes stronger.

### Lead / Beachhead Scene

A narrower scene where users are especially sensitive to the problem or willing to adopt earlier.

Use it to identify an initial user group or a strong early use case. Do not automatically assume the beachhead scene represents the total market.

### Extreme Scene

A high-stress or visually strong scene that exposes product limits and can reveal engineering requirements.

Use extreme scenes mainly to discover reliability, performance, safety, or robustness requirements. Do not let an extreme case over-specify a mainstream product without evidence.

## Scene Hierarchy Rule

Do not give every scene equal weight.

For each candidate scene ask:

- Is this the reason the user buys the product?
- Is it merely an occasional use case?
- Is it useful for early adoption?
- Is it mainly a stress test?

This prevents feature creep caused by trying to satisfy every possible context.

## Scene Change

Scenes can change because of:

- new technology
- new user habits
- new devices
- new channel behavior
- regulation
- remote work / mobility changes
- ecosystem shifts

A stable user need can lose commercial value if the scene that triggers the behavior disappears or changes.

Therefore competitive and user research should monitor not only product changes, but scene changes.
